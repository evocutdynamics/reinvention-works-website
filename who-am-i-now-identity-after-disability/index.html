Who Am I Now? Rebuilding Your Identity After Vision Loss or Disability

November 17, 2026 · Shay Edmonds

There is a question that shows up after the diagnosis, after the hospital, after the appointments slow down. Nobody hands you a pamphlet for it. It sounds like this: who am I now?

I know that question well. I spent my career as a 911 emergency communications engineer, the person who kept critical systems running when everything else was falling apart. Then, as an adult, I lost most of my sight to a rare autoimmune condition called Anti-MOG. I’m legally blind. The medical side of that was hard. But the part that nearly took me apart was quieter: I didn’t know who I was anymore.

If you’re living with vision loss, a brain injury, a stroke, a chronic illness or any disability that arrived in the middle of your life, this guide is for you. It isn’t medical advice and it isn’t therapy. It’s what I’ve learned, rebuilding my own life and walking alongside others doing the same.

Why disability can shake your identity so deeply

Most of us build our sense of self from three things: what we do, what we can count on, and how other people see us. An acquired disability can disrupt all three at once.

  • What you do. A job, a role in your family, a hobby, the way you got around. When those change, it can feel like the person attached to them disappeared too.
  • What you can count on. Your body, your energy, your independence. Things that used to be automatic now take planning.
  • How others see you. People start talking to you differently. Some pull away. Some hover. You can feel like you’ve been moved into a new category without being asked.

That’s why “just stay positive” rarely helps. You aren’t struggling because you lack a good attitude. You’re struggling because the foundation you built your identity on actually moved.

Grief is part of this, not a detour from it

Many people are surprised by how much grief comes with disability. Not only for what you lost, but for the future you had planned: the career path, the retirement, the version of you that was going to do certain things.

That grief is normal. It also isn’t a straight line. You can feel at peace one week and furious the next. Rebuilding doesn’t mean pretending the loss didn’t happen. It means making room for it while you figure out what comes next.

Step 1: Separate what’s true now from the story you’re telling yourself

After a major change, facts and fears get tangled together. “I can’t drive anymore” is a fact. “So I’m a burden to everyone” is a story. Stories feel just as real as facts, but they’re much heavier, and they’re often wrong.

Try this: write or record two lists. On one, only things you know for certain about your situation today. On the other, the conclusions you’ve drawn from them. Then look at the second list and ask, honestly, which of those you actually know. Most people find their story is harsher than their facts.

I wrote more about this in Redefining Success After Disability Changes Everything.

Step 2: Rebuild around the energy you actually have

One of the biggest traps after disability is measuring yourself against the energy you used to have. Everything takes more effort now: navigating, adapting, explaining, recovering. That isn’t laziness. It’s a real cost, and it’s invisible to most people around you.

Start paying attention to your actual capacity, day by day. What drains you? What restores you? What time of day do you think most clearly? Plans built on your real energy hold up. Plans built on the energy you wish you had collapse, and then you blame yourself.

If this one hits home, read Why Exhaustion Isn’t Laziness.

Step 3: Choose one next step that fits who you are today

You don’t need a five-year plan right now. You need one step that fits your life as it actually is. Small, specific and doable this week.

That might be learning one new accessibility tool, reconnecting with one person, trying one class, or telling one person the truth about how you’re doing. Each step you complete gives you something identity is built on: evidence that you can still act on your own life.

You’re not starting over. You’re starting from experience.

It can feel like everything you were has been wiped out. It hasn’t. Your skills, your values, your humor, the way you solve problems: those came with you. In my case, years of keeping emergency systems running taught me how to stay steady under pressure and work the problem in front of me. That turned out to matter more after I lost my sight, not less.

Make a list of what you’ve always been good at that doesn’t depend on what changed. That list is the beginning of your next chapter. More on this in You’re Not Starting Over, You’re Starting From Experience.

What tends to help, and what doesn’t

What helps:

  • Spending time with people who have been through something similar. Being understood without having to explain is powerful.
  • Routines and tools that make daily life more accessible, so your energy goes to living, not just managing.
  • Adjusting your identity rather than trying to erase the old one or freeze it in place.
  • Being honest about bad days instead of performing “fine.”

What usually doesn’t:

  • Forced positivity and “everything happens for a reason.”
  • Comparing your progress to who you were before.
  • Waiting until you feel completely ready to take any step at all.

Many of the patterns that look like self-sabotage during this time are actually protection: your mind trying to keep you safe from more loss. I go deep on that in my book, Stop the Self Sabotage: You’re Not Broken, You’re Unfinished.

When to reach for more support

Rebuilding identity sits alongside medical and mental health care. It doesn’t replace it. If you’re feeling hopeless most days, can’t function, or are having thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor or a licensed mental health professional. If you’re in crisis in the U.S., call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

Questions people ask

Is it normal to feel like I lost myself after becoming disabled?

Yes. Losing roles, independence and familiar routines can make it feel like your identity went with them. It’s one of the most common experiences after an acquired disability, and one of the least talked about.

How long does it take to rebuild your identity after disability?

There’s no set timeline. For many people it unfolds over months or years, and it isn’t a straight line. What matters more than speed is having a way forward and people who understand.

Can I rebuild a career after vision loss?

Many people do, sometimes in the same field with new tools and accommodations, sometimes in a new direction that draws on the same strengths. I rebuilt my own career after losing my sight, and it started with knowing what I still brought to the table.
