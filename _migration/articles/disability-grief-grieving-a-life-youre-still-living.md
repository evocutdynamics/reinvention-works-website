# Disability Grief: Why You Grieve a Life You're Still Living

*Disability grief rarely looks like grief. It looks like frustration, anger, and holding on too tight, sometimes for years, until someone finally calls it what it is.*

*About an 8-minute read.*

For a long time, my friends kept riding.

Before my vision went, I rode dirt bikes. Motocross wasn't a hobby for me. It was my identity, my lifestyle, my passion, and the way I expressed myself. And it died right alongside my vision.

Afterward, I'd hear about my friends' weekend rides, the trails they took and the beers they drank after, and something in me would tighten.

I don't know that jealousy is the right word. It was more like envy. Or, honestly, just grief. The grief of not being a part of it anymore.

I didn't call it grief back then. I didn't have the word for it. I was grieving a life I was still living. I still had the same personality. But I was not the same person.

## Disability grief doesn't look like grief

When people picture grief, they picture a funeral, crying, and casseroles on the counter. Disability grief rarely looks like that, so a lot of people don't recognize it, including the person going through it.

Mine looked like frustration. I was angry, I was cynical, and I felt like a victim to everything. Why me? Why now? And why does it keep happening?

I spent somewhere around 14 to 16 years in that place. I didn't understand that it was grief. I thought I was just frustrated, maybe just a frustrated person now. I felt very alone and isolated.

If you've been irritable for months or years, if small things set you off, or if you keep comparing who you are now to who you used to be, it's worth asking a different question. Not "what's wrong with me?" but "what am I grieving?"

## You're grieving someone who's still at the table

Grief after a death has a shape people recognize. Grief after a disability is stranger, because the person you're missing is you.

You still wake up in the same bed and answer to the same name. You still laugh at the same jokes. But the version of you who could do certain things, hold a certain job, play a certain role in your family, isn't coming back in that form. And nobody brings a casserole for that.

People around you often don't know what to do with it either. You'll hear "don't let it stop you" or "it could be worse." Sure, it could be worse. It could also be a whole lot better, and nobody ever seems to lead with that one. Those lines come from a good place, but they skip right past the loss.

Plenty of people living with a disability know the feeling of being told to move on from something nobody acknowledged in the first place.

## Why you hold on to things that hurt you

This is the part of my grief I'm least proud of, and I think it's the part a lot of people don't get to say out loud.

After enough losses, I got so desperate not to lose anything else that I held on to things that weren't healthy for me. I just couldn't handle one more loss, so I kept hold of whatever I still had, even when it was hurting me.

If you're doing that, look at it as grief protecting itself. When you've already lost a lot, letting go of anything, even something bad for you, can feel like more of the same.

## Grieving the future you'd planned

Disability grief isn't only about what you could do before. It's also about the future you had mapped out: the career you were building, the trips you'd talked about, the way you pictured being a parent or a partner years from now.

That loss is easy to dismiss, even for yourself, because none of it had happened yet. But a plan you'd been living toward is real, and losing it is real. If someone tells you "it was just a job" or "you can still travel," they may be right about the facts and still miss what you lost.

For me, every one of those losses took a future I'd been counting on with it. For years, I didn't grieve any of them. I just got angrier.

## It doesn't happen once

A lot of people expect grief to have an end. You feel it, you work through it, and it's done.

Disability grief often comes back. A new symptom, a new limit, a birthday or a milestone you can't take part in the way you'd planned, a friend's news that lands differently than it would have before. Each one can open it up again.

My own vision has gone completely dark a few times and then come back. Each time, the grief started over. That's common with conditions that change, flare, or progress: the loss isn't one event, it's a series of them.

So if you thought you'd finished grieving and it's back, something changed, and grief is responding to it.

## Naming it is what lets it move

Grief you don't recognize tends to come out sideways: as anger at people who didn't cause it, as cynicism about everything, as holding on too tight.

For me, it also came out as the opposite of holding on. I'd tolerate something for as long as I could, and then one day I couldn't anymore, and I'd burn the whole situation down.

Not because burning it down fixed anything. Because it was the one thing I could control. For a moment, I was a participant who mattered, and a voice that was finally heard.

Once I could call it grief, I could start working with it instead of being run by it.

A few things helped me, and they might help you.

**Call it what it is.** The next time frustration flares, ask what loss is underneath it. You don't have to fix it. Just name it, even if only to yourself.

**Say it to one person.** Grief carried in silence gets heavier. Telling one person who can hold it, a friend, a counselor, or someone who has lived something similar, doesn't make it disappear, but it usually makes it lighter to carry.

**Separate grief from the story about it.** "I lost the ability to ride" is grief. "I'm useless now" is a story built on top of it. The first deserves to be felt. The second deserves a second look.

**Let grief and new things share the space.** I still grieve old friends and old relationships, and the memories of who I used to be and what I could do. That doesn't mean I can't do new things now. Both can be true in the same week, sometimes the same afternoon.

If the grief starts to feel like it's swallowing everything, or you're struggling to stay safe, reach out for real-time support. That's a sign you need more help than any article can give, not a sign you're grieving wrong.

## Grief before acceptance

For me, there was grief before the acceptance came. I had to stop calling it frustration and let it be what it was before I could do anything else with it.

That's where the next question lives: what does acceptance actually mean, if it doesn't mean being happy about it? I wrote about that in [How to Accept a Disability Without Pretending You're Fine](/how-to-accept-a-disability-without-pretending-youre-fine/). I won't pretend the grief ends. I still feel it. It just doesn't run my life the way it used to.
