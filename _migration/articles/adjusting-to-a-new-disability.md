# Somebody Moved the Kitchen: Adjusting to a New Disability

*Adjusting to a new disability isn't getting back to the old plan. It's finding out the plan itself changed, and drawing a new one you can actually live in.*

*About an 8-minute read.*

Picture building a house from a blueprint you've trusted for years. You know where every wall goes. Then, without telling you, somebody moves the kitchen.

You keep building anyway. The plumbing runs to a room that isn't there. The cabinets don't fit. Every wall you put up comes out a little wrong, and you can't figure out why, because you're still reading the old plan.

That's what adjusting to a new disability felt like to me. Not one loss. A plan that quietly stopped matching the house.

## How mine started

It explains the rest, so I'll start there.

One morning I woke up and couldn't see out of the right side of my right eye. The doctor told me it usually won't jump to the other eye. It did. For more than a decade after that, nobody could tell me what it was.

So I did what a lot of people do. I waited for it to sort itself out. My immune system would figure it out, or the doctors would. I called it hope. Looking back, I wasn't in denial, exactly. I knew something was wrong. I just didn't think it would last as long as it has.

Then things started falling off the tracks. That's when it hit me that this wasn't only about my vision. It was about everything I'd built on top of it.

Maybe yours started differently: a diagnosis, an injury, a slow change that finally got a name. The details change from person to person. That moment when the old blueprint stops working tends to show up for a lot of people, whatever the cause.

## Why everything you build keeps failing

I wish someone had told me this early. When a disability changes your life, the first instinct is to keep living the old way. Same job, same pace, same roles, same idea of who you are. Just try harder.

It doesn't work, and it isn't because you're weak or lazy. You're running a life designed for a situation you don't have anymore.

I spent years as an engineer, and when a system keeps failing, I go looking for the design flaw. Mine turned out to be simple: I kept asking my new life to run on my old specs.

In my head, it went like this. I could work harder to make up for what I couldn't do anymore. I could fake how involved I was, like at my son's soccer games.

At work, I could put in extra hours when nobody was around to make up for my vision. I could hold on tighter to the friendships and relationships that were drifting away. I could find a way to show up exactly like I did before.

Most of it didn't work, at least not for long. I was faking it with the people I cared about. I wasn't getting what I really needed. And I was exhausted from all the compensating. The whole time, I was still losing.

So the failures stacked up. I lost my vision. I lost my career. I lost my marriage, and the ability to parent the way I wanted to. I lost friends. I lost a new career, then another business. I tried day trading. It went about as good as it sounds.

At one point I was working in produce at the same grocery store where I'd bagged groceries as a teenager. The people there were great.

The hard part was running into people who'd known the old me, before the disability and before all the losses, in full public view. A lot of people know some version of that moment, and almost nobody talks about it.

## The stages of adjustment to disability don't go in order

If you've searched this topic, you've probably found the stage lists. Shock, denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance, in that order, like steps on a staircase.

Those lists can be useful for naming feelings. But adjustment rarely goes in order, and if you expect it to, you can start thinking you're doing it wrong.

For a lot of people it's more like weather than stairs. You can feel angry and accepting in the same week. You can think you've made peace with something, then a new loss shows up and you're back at the start.

Progressive and fluctuating conditions make this messier, because the house keeps changing while you're trying to redraw the blueprint.

So skip the question "what stage am I in?" A more useful question is "what's actually true right now, and what am I still doing the old way?" (My own anger, frustration and cynicism, it turned out, were grief I didn't recognize. That's a whole piece of its own: [Disability Grief: Why You Grieve a Life You're Still Living](/disability-grief-grieving-a-life-youre-still-living/).)

## Nobody on your team owns this part

The medical staff I worked with were tremendous. Therapy was tremendous. And still, nobody was helping me with who I was supposed to be in my own life.

That isn't a knock on them. Doctors are there to stabilize your body. Therapy helps with what's happening in your mind, and some therapy does reach into these areas. Support groups help too, by putting you in a room with people who get it.

But none of them exactly has the job of helping you rebuild the rest: your work, your relationships, what you do with a Saturday, who you are now.

So a lot of people end up doing it alone, or not at all. Some keep living the old way for years. I did.

The question underneath all of it is the one I wrote about in [Who Am I Now? Identity After Disability](/who-am-i-now-identity-after-disability/). Adjusting is where that question turns into practical work.

## The people around you are adjusting too

It took me too long to see that my disability didn't only change my life. It changed things for everyone who was built into it.

I knew I'd lose some things I loved. I didn't realize what it would do to my relationships. My marriage took a beating. Some friendships faded, and a few got stronger.

Your partner, your kids, your friends and the people you work with are all still working from an old version of you, too. Some of them will adjust with you. Some will struggle, and some won't stay. That's one of the hardest parts of this, and it's worth saying out loud rather than finding out one loss at a time.

It may not feel fair. But some people simply won't get it, and some can't find their own footing in what changed. When someone drifts away, it doesn't always mean they didn't care. Sometimes they just couldn't find a way to stay.

## Drawing a new blueprint

You don't redraw a whole house in one sitting. Start with a few honest measurements.

**Write down what's actually true now.** Not what you're hoping comes back, and not the worst-case story you tell yourself at 2 a.m. What your body does today, what your energy looks like on a typical week, and what changed for good versus what might still change. Some of it may improve. Some may not. Either way, you can't build well on a guess.

**Find the walls you're still building from the old plan.** Look for the places where frustration keeps piling up. Maybe you're forcing a job to work the way it used to, or you quit a hobby entirely because you can't do it the old way. That's usually where the old blueprint is still running.

**Ask where the kitchen goes now.** Some things you loved can come back in a different form. Some need to be grieved and let go. A few new things may show up that the old plan never had room for. You won't know which is which until you look.

**Count the cost honestly.** Money, work and family don't pause while you figure this out. Maybe there's a paycheck you lost, bills that keep coming, a partner or kids who still need you. A new blueprint has to account for those, along with the barriers you didn't design: buildings, jobs and systems that weren't built with you in mind. Name those as facts to work around.

None of this is fast. Simple, but not easy, and people who know me will tell you I'm stubborn. I'd call it persistent. Adjusting takes some of both.

## How long does adjusting to a disability take?

There isn't a timeline, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. Some people move quickly. Others move slowly. Both are normal.

A better measure than time is this: are you still trying to live the old way, or have you started building around what's true now? You can be early in the process years after a diagnosis, or well along in it a few months in.

## What I fill it with now

Eventually, I'd had enough. I stopped trying to force the old kitchen back where it was. I was ready to face the truth and move through the changes that would come with it, and I understood that more loss would probably be part of that.

What I didn't expect was the relief. The relief of being myself again, for real. I got to rebuild with the same person I'd always been, this time around what I can actually do now.

I stopped being a victim of my circumstances and started running the show. I came out of the shadows and into my own spotlight. I started living through what I can do, instead of hiding in shame over what I can't.

I'm not going to pretend that ended the hard days. I still struggle with my vision every day.

But I did get to build a new kitchen. I have a new pantry now, and I get to ask a question I never had room to ask before: what do I want to fill it with?

That's the question waiting at the end of adjusting to a new disability. Not "how do I get the old life back," but "what goes in the space I have now?"
