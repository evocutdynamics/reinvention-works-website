How to Accept a Disability Without Pretending You're Fine

December 1, 2026 · Shay Edmonds

Acceptance doesn't mean you're happy about it. It means you stop pointing the camera away from what happened, so you can finally build from what's real.

About an 8-minute read.

At my kids' games, I couldn't follow what was happening on the field.

I used to coach my son's soccer team, so some of those parents had watched my decline up close. When I stepped away from coaching, the teams changed and the parents changed, but the story stayed. Every new season was a new reason to build anxiety around a story that was already set in concrete in my head.

Other parents would cheer, and I'd stay quiet, because I didn't know what had just happened. I didn't want to tell every parent on the sideline, "I can't see, that's why I'm not cheering." So I said nothing.

In my head, it was a no-win game. If I cheered and it turned out the other team had scored, I looked stupid. If I didn't cheer when our kids did something great, I looked lame, like a dad who didn't care enough to support them. A storyline where I lose no matter what.

But that's all it was: a story I was making up. Well, mostly making up.

That's what pretending you're fine looks like for a lot of people. Not a big lie. Just a lot of small silences, so nobody has to know how much has changed.

What acceptance isn't

A lot of people hear "accept your disability" and cringe, and I understand why. It can sound like being told to give up, to stop hoping, or to smile about something that hurts.

That isn't what it meant for me. I was living in a lot of denial, and I had to accept what had happened.

Acceptance doesn't mean you're happy about it. It means you accept the reality of what it truly is. And once I got there, I could also accept that I could create from that point. That's where things changed for me.

So if you're not happy about your disability, you don't have to be.

Turn the camera toward what's real

I think about it this way. If you change the direction the camera's pointing, it changes the entire story.

For years, my camera pointed away. Away from the parts of my vision I'd lost, away from what that meant for my work and my family, away from anything that made it permanent. I kept filming the life I used to have and wondering why the story didn't make sense anymore.

Acceptance was turning the camera toward what was actually there. Not the worst version, and not the hopeful version. The real one.

That sounds like it would make things worse, and for a little while it can. But you can't build anything from a picture you won't look at. Once the camera points at the truth, you can start deciding what happens next in the story.

Grief comes first

I want to be honest about the order. For me, there was grief before the acceptance came.

If you try to skip straight to acceptance, it usually turns into pretending: saying "I've made my peace with it" while the grief runs underneath everything you do. Real acceptance tends to come after you've let yourself feel what you lost. (I wrote about that part in Disability Grief: Why You Grieve a Life You're Still Living.)

When acceptance finally came

For me, acceptance came when I got tired. Tired of being my own worst enemy. Tired of living a life that wasn't really mine, spending my energy taking care of how other people felt about me, on the sideline and everywhere else.

I'd been running on two speeds: put up with it, or blow it up. Neither one ever got me what I actually needed. (I wrote about where that comes from in the grief piece.)

The hardest truth to swallow was this. Something real had happened to me, and none of it was my choice. But I was the one keeping it going. Every day, I picked up the same old story and carried it a little further.

I'd made my world so small and so careful that there was barely any of me left in it. It all came to a head at a point where I could have lost everything at once.

I could have kept living that way. Plenty of people do, for years. Instead, I faced my reality. And I didn't do it by gritting my teeth and white-knuckling it. I did it with grace, and with a plan.

That plan is the same one I use today with individuals, families, and medical staff.

Accepting what might still change

One of the hardest parts of acceptance for me wasn't accepting what had already happened. It was accepting what still could.

Once I started to accept my situation, I was also accepting that I might lose another friend, or another romantic partner. I wasn't happy about it. But I was ready.

Something unexpected came with that. The friends who stayed became closer to me. I didn't have to manage what they thought of me anymore, so I could be more myself around them. When you stop pretending, some people drift. The ones who stay get to know the real you.

When people want you to be inspiring

There's another kind of pretending, and it comes from the outside. People sometimes want your disability to be a story about bravery. "You're so strong." "I could never handle what you handle."

That strength is real. But for a lot of people living with a disability, being brave was never a choice. Nobody asked if you wanted to be strong. You just had to be, because no other option existed.

So the compliment is meant kindly, and it's true, but it can leave you feeling like you're not allowed to have a bad day in front of them.

Accepting your disability doesn't mean becoming anyone's inspiration. It means being honest about the good days and the hard ones, in front of the people who matter.

And if your condition changes over time, acceptance isn't something you do once. Each change can ask you to accept a new reality. The move is the same every time: point the camera at what's actually there.

How to accept a disability without pretending you're fine

None of this is a checklist you finish. I still practice all of it, some days better than others.

Say the true sentence, out loud, to one person. Pretending lives in silence. Saying what's real to someone you trust, "I can't follow the game anymore," or "I can't work the way I used to," is often the first crack in it.

One caution on that second example: telling an employer about a disability is a bigger decision, with real pros and cons, and it's worth knowing your options first. In the US, the Job Accommodation Network (askjan.org) offers free, confidential guidance on disclosure and workplace accommodations.

Tell people what you need instead of hoping they guess. I felt like a burden a lot, so I wouldn't ask for things. That kept me quiet at the games and plenty of other places. People can't read your mind any better than I can read their faces.

Ask instead of assuming. I can't always read a face and know whether someone's frustrated with me or happy with me. Even happiness can go unnoticed. So I've learned to ask.

Decide what you're responsible for. I'm not responsible for the way other people feel about what's happening to me. I am responsible for my own happiness, and for surrounding myself with the right people. That line took me a long time to believe.

Expect barriers, and don't take them personally. Some places, jobs and systems weren't built with you in mind. Accepting your disability doesn't mean accepting every barrier around it. It means seeing them clearly enough to push on the ones you can.

Stop asking when the losing stops

For a long time, my question was "I've already lost enough. When is this losing going to stop?"

That question kept me stuck, because I had no control over the answer. Acceptance let me flip the coin. Instead of waiting for the losses to end, I started coming from a place of ownership and accountability: what can I do with what's actually here?

I still struggle daily with my vision. Some days I want to play small and hide in the shadows, because showing up means stepping out of my comfort zone. Acceptance didn't take that away. It just means those days don't get to decide everything.

And some days I can laugh about it. My daughter once told me that if I were a rap star, I'd be named Blurry Man. Acceptance leaves room for that kind of laugh, too.

Point the camera this week

Pick one place where you're still pretending you're fine. Maybe it's a sideline, a job, a family dinner, or a conversation you keep avoiding. Point the camera at what's real there, and say one true thing about it to one person.

Acceptance doesn't end there, but that's where the new story starts.
